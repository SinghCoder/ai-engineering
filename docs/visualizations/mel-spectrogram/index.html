How sound becomes
a mel spectrogram

Listen for three sounds in this clip. A low hum gives way to a hiss, then a rising tone. Slide the blue window over the waveform to see what a short stretch of sound reveals.

From a window to a column

A Fourier transform estimates the power at each frequency inside the selected 25 ms window. Take a spectrum every 10 ms and line the columns up. The hum leaves horizontal lines at its steady frequencies. The rising tone leaves a diagonal trace as its frequency climbs.

What a mel band keeps

Mel bands pool neighboring frequencies. Low bands cover narrow ranges in hertz, while high bands cover wider ranges. The spectrogram below shows the same clip after that pooling. Pick a cell to see exactly which frequencies contribute to its value.

The filter gives frequencies near the band center more weight. Add the weighted powers, then take the logarithm. Repeat this for all 80 bands and you get one feature vector. Some speech models, including Mel-LLM, project that sequence into the language model’s input space.

The price of a longer window

With more samples in each window, two nearby tones are easier to separate. Try the click next. The same longer window spreads that brief event across more frames.

Calculation notes

The clip is generated at 16 kHz and lasts 2.4 seconds. Frames use centered Hann windows, with zero padding at the ends and a 1,024-point FFT. The FFT powers are unnormalized. Triangular filters are spaced on the HTK mel scale. Each heatmap uses its own peak as the 0 dB reference. The window comparisons share a reference within each pair.
