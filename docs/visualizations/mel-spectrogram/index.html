How sound becomes a mel spectrogram

Play the clip. You’ll hear a hum, a hiss, and a rising tone. The blue window starts in the hum. Drag it into the hiss. What happens to the peaks beneath it?

Line up the spectra

Look at the hum in the plot below. Its peaks stay at the same frequencies, so they form horizontal lines. The rising tone leaves a diagonal trace as its peak moves up. We get this spectrogram by moving the 25 ms window in 10 ms steps and using a Fourier transform to calculate a spectrum at each step.

Which frequencies go into a mel band?

Pick a cell near the bottom of the plot, then pick one near the top. Read the frequency range beside the Mel band label. Each mel band combines neighboring frequencies, and higher bands span wider ranges.

The triangle peaks at the center of the band. Multiply each bin’s power by the height of the triangle at that frequency, then add the products and take the logarithm. Do this for all 80 bands and you have one feature vector for this instant. Speech models such as Mel-LLM, project a sequence of these vectors into the language model’s input space.

Calculation notes

We generate 2.4 seconds of audio at 16 kHz. For each frame, we apply a centered Hann window, pad with zeros, and compute a 1,024-point FFT. We square the magnitudes to get power and space the triangular filters on the HTK mel scale. Each heatmap uses its own peak as the 0 dB reference. Each pair in the window comparison shares a reference.
